Teaching an aircraft to fly to a goal, one small update at a time

1The competition task

Fly an A320 to a goal. Stay clear of other aircraft, restricted areas and the edge of the sector. You only command heading and speed; BlueSky does the flying.

Rules of the world

Goal captured within 5 km. Loss of separation below 5 NM. Episode truncates at 3000 s. Nothing but reaching the goal ends an episode early: hitting an obstacle or another aircraft is only counted.

What is scored

Goal reached · intrusions (events & seconds) · restricted-area entries · sector exits · flight time. Reported over the first 1000 episodes of seed 42.

What you design

The observation, the reward, the action mapping — and the algorithm, as long as it is reinforcement learning. The scoring layer and the world's rules are fixed.

Single-agent track

One aircraft, 10 scripted intruders on fixed routes, 5 obstacles. The testing ground.

Multi-agent track

10 learning aircraft, 5 obstacles, no scripted traffic. The high-value scoring goal.

5The policy is a bell curve over the action

For each state the agent outputs a mean μ(s) and a width σ(s). It then samples its heading command from that bell. The command a means: turn by a × 45° from the current heading (positive = right), clipped to ±1.

Early in training σ is wide (try 0.5): the aircraft tries many headings. Later it narrows (try 0.1): nearly deterministic. Nothing forces that. Noisy steering costs reward, so the gradient narrows it.

6Features: how a state becomes numbers

The policy and the critic are linear in a long vector x(s) that is mostly zeros. Tile coding builds it: several shifted grids lie over the input space, and a state lights up exactly one tile in each. Drag the point.

number of grids

Each grid is the same size but shifted a little, so their overlap is finer than any single tile. With 1 grid the picture is blocky; with 8 (what we use) the active region shrinks to a small neighbourhood around the point.

In our code the groups are: goal (distance, bearing) · obstacle (clearance, bearing) · obstacle + goal · intruder (distance, bearing, aspect) · closest approach (time and miss distance). Every angle is relative to the aircraft's own heading, so "steer toward the goal" is learned once and works anywhere — the sector's shape changes every episode.

7The learning rule: one update, in slow motion

After every step the critic compares what happened to what it predicted. That surprise is the TD error δ = R + v(S') − v(S). The actor then moves its bell toward the action it tried if δ is positive, away from it if negative.

μ  ← μ  + αμ · δ · (a − μ)
log σ ← log σ + ασ · δ · ((a−μ)²/σ² − 1)        αμ=0.3, ασ=0.02

This is the net effect of one update on one state's μ and σ; in the real code the change is spread over the ~8–48 active tiles. Try a positive δ with an action far from μ: the bell slides toward it. With a negative δ it slides away. The width update widens the bell when an unusual action turns out well, and narrows it when it turns out badly.

8The reward, and the lesson about penalties

The agent only ever optimises the reward we write. Per decision step (in seconds-equivalent; the code divides by 100):

reward = −(seconds flown)                  every second costs 1
       + (distance closed) / speed          progress toward the goal (potential-based shaping)
       + 500 if the goal is reached
       − 1 × (seconds in a restricted area, outside the sector, or in loss of separation)
       − E × (new violation events)            E = the "event penalty"

The progress term is potential-based shaping: it gives feedback on every step and, with γ = 1, provably does not change which policy is best. The violation terms come straight from the competition's own scored metrics.

Would the agent clip the obstacle corner?

Drag the sliders. The agent picks whichever option has the better (less negative) total. The defaults are illustrative: the detour is roughly what the canonical layout cost (535 s vs. 462 s), the 15 s inside is a guess.

What actually happened. Our first stage-2 run used E = 0.5. The policy learned to clip the obstacle corner in 25% of random scenarios, because a short clip cost less than the detour it saved. That is the reward working as written, not a bug. Raising E to 5 removed the clipping entirely (0%), at a cost of about 9 seconds of flight time.

12Where this sits among policy-gradient methods

First, a clarification: what we built is a policy-gradient method. The contrast is between kinds of method, and between this implementation and the PPO the competition ships.

Reinforcement learning
Value-based learn q(s,a), act greedily
Sarsa, Q-learning, DQN — your gridworld work; needs discrete actions
Policy-gradient learn the policy directly
REINFORCE waits for the episode's return: noisy
+ baseline subtract v(s) to cut noise
Actor-critic, one step ← what we built
Deep variants: A2C, PPO, SAC same ideas, neural nets, batches

Why a critic?

REINFORCE multiplies the gradient by the whole episode's return, so one good step is drowned in the noise of everything else. The critic gives a low-noise "better or worse than expected" signal on every step, at the cost of a little bias.

Why not Sarsa?

Our heading command is continuous. Value-based methods want a small list of actions; a policy distribution can be sampled directly.

The PPO column describes the settings in the competition guide and Stable-Baselines3's usual behaviour; we have not run it here. Both methods are on-policy: the policy is optimised for how it behaves with its own noise on, but we evaluate it without noise (the mean action). Neither guarantees the best policy; both climb to a local optimum.

13What is still missing, and a self-check

  1. Scale. Half-size, 1 obstacle, 1 intruder, versus 5 and 10 and much larger sectors. Not evaluated on the seed-42 harness.
  2. Ten intruders. Our features see only the nearest one. Ten (and nine other learners) need richer features or a neural network. The update rule carries over unchanged.
  3. Speed is held fixed; the competition allows learning it.
  4. Intrusions are not at zero (about 7%), and obstacle and intruder avoidance are separate feature groups, so the policy cannot trade one against the other.
  5. Multi-agent is the stated high-value target and is not started.
  6. Novelty and the report. This is a clear, explainable baseline and a good teaching tool; by itself it is not an original contribution.
Why a Gaussian policy instead of a list of headings?

The heading command is continuous, and a distribution can be sampled and differentiated directly.

What is δ, and what does the actor do with it?

How much better or worse the step went than the critic predicted. The actor makes the tried action more likely if δ > 0 and less likely if δ < 0.

Why does σ shrink?

Noisy steering costs reward, so the gradient narrows it. Nothing forces it; the policy sharpens because sharpening pays.

Why are all angles relative to the aircraft's heading?

The scenario changes every episode, so a rule has to hold anywhere. Absolute coordinates would not transfer.

Why doesn't the progress reward change the best policy?

It is potential-based shaping with potential −d/v: feedback on every step without changing which policy has the best return (exact for γ = 1).

What caused the 25% obstacle entries?

A penalty smaller than the detour cost: the agent followed the reward. A larger event penalty fixed it.

What has to change to reach the competition?

Handle 10 intruders and 5 obstacles (richer features or a neural network), learn speed, run at full scale, then multi-agent; and evaluate on the official seed-42 harness.
