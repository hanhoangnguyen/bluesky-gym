Contextual stochastic optimization: deciding well when the future is uncertain but you have clues

A plain-language tour of the idea your professor pointed to: what it is, how it differs from control theory and reinforcement learning, what it could (and could not) do for dynamic obstacles that scale, and where to read more. Everything marked interactive runs in your browser.

What it is

Pick a decision z now to minimise the expected cost, given what you observed: E[ f(z, x) | y ]. You see y (today's temperature). You cannot see x yet (tomorrow's demand). The link between them is only statistical, learned from data.

Why it might help us

A moving intruder is exactly this shape: we observe its position and velocity (y); where it will be (x) is uncertain. The framework says: predict a distribution, price the risk in the cost, and, for scale, note that expected cost adds up over obstacles.

Honest caveats

The single-agent intruders are mostly predictable (data in section 5). In the multi-agent track others react to us, which breaks the framework's key assumption. And the competition requires an RL-based solution, so this would feed RL, not replace it.

1The idea in one equation

Your notes, restated. You measure something y. There is no exact formula from y to the thing you care about, x; there is only a loose probabilistic connection. You still have to decide z before x is revealed.

minimizez   E[ f(z, x)  |  y ]
y · the context

What you observe now. Today's temperature. Also called side information, features or covariates.

x · the uncertain outcome

What you cannot see yet and the cost depends on. Tomorrow's ice-cream demand. Only its conditional distribution given y is available (from past pairs (yi, xi)).

z · the decision

What you choose. How many tubs to order. f(z, x) is the cost of having chosen z once x turns out to be what it is.

Heads-up on letters. Most papers swap the roles: the context is called x and the uncertain outcome is called y (or ξ). I use the letters from your notes (y observed, x uncertain). When you read Bertsimas & Kallus or the Sadana et al. survey, translate.

My reading: CSO is the "one decision, uncertainty I cannot influence" special case of RL, with one extra gift: the cost function f is known, so after you see x you can score every decision you could have made, not just the one you took. The 2025 survey by Sadana et al. (section 8) notes that training a parameterised policy was already standard in RL under the name policy-gradient methods.

2The ice-cream lab

You sell ice cream. Each morning you see the temperature y and must order tubs z before knowing the day's demand x. Order too little and you lose sales; order too much and it melts. Below, five ways to decide are compared on the same fake data. Drag today's temperature and watch the decisions move.

Try this. Set "stock-out costs × more" to 1 (leftovers and lost sales cost the same). The forecast-the-mean method is now nearly as good as the rest. Raise it to 5: ordering the average demand leaves you short half the time, and its cost jumps.
And this. Switch the relationship to S-curve (demand saturates on hot days). The straight-line methods miss the bend; the neighbour method follows it. Then try no relationship: the clue is useless, and ignoring it is hard to beat.
The lesson to remember. The best order is a quantile of the demand distribution, not its average: with stock-out cost k times leftover cost, order enough to cover demand with probability k / (1 + k). A point forecast throws away exactly the information (the spread, and which side of it hurts more) that the decision needs. The same is true for aircraft: being inside 5 NM is far worse than being 20 NM away, so how likely a conflict is matters more than where the intruder will be on average.

3A map of the methods

All of them try to approximate the same thing, the best decision for each value of y. They differ in what they learn and how much work happens at decision time. The survey by Sadana et al. groups the literature into three frameworks: decision-rule optimization, sequential learning and optimization (SLO) and integrated learning and optimization (ILO). The lab above uses the first four rows.

Two extras worth knowing. Robust versions replace "expected cost" by "worst case over a plausible set of outcomes", where the set depends on y (Chenreddy, Bandi & Delage 2022). Conformal prediction wraps a predictor with regions that contain the truth with a chosen probability, which gives safety guarantees for planning among moving agents (Lindemann et al. 2023).

4From ice cream to aircraft

The translation is direct. A moving intruder is a demand you cannot see yet.

Left to right: we observe the intruder (y); its possible futures form a fan (x given y), here some bending off the straight line; we then score each candidate heading by the share of futures in which it ends within the separation limit (the dashed circle) and choose a low-risk one with a small detour (z). The colours in the right panel are computed from the same fan.

Why "exogenous" matters. CSO assumes that what I decide does not change what happens. For the single-agent track that is true by construction (intruders fly fixed routes with BlueSky's own resolution off). It gives us two things: a clean statistical problem, and the ability to replay logged intruder paths and score every heading we could have chosen. In the multi-agent track the other aircraft are learners reacting to us; that is a game, and plain CSO is the wrong tool without extra machinery.

6The scaling idea: a risk profile that does not grow with the number of obstacles

Your professor's question was a scalable method for dynamic obstacles. Here is the CSO-flavoured answer, as a toy you can run. For every candidate heading we estimate the probability of a conflict against each intruder's distribution of futures, then combine. The result is a fixed-size vector (13 numbers) however many intruders there are.

use only the most urgent

Why it scales

The expected cost of a decision adds up over obstacles: E[Σi penaltyi] = Σi E[penaltyi], even if the obstacles are correlated. Each obstacle's risk depends only on its own context. So the work is N × K headings × M samples × T time steps: linear in N. For the chance of any conflict I assume independent intruders, 1 − Π(1 − pi) (a union bound Σ pi works without independence).

What would blow up, and what does not

Enumerating every combination of every intruder's possible future would need MN cases: with M=100, N=12 that is 1024. Monte-Carlo does not do that; it samples. What does grow is (i) the work, linearly in N, and (ii) the samples needed to see a rare conflict (roughly 1/p). Dynamic programming over the joint state, or scenario trees over many steps, are where the exponential lives.

This is a toy: straight-line intruders with an optional single bend (like the generator's routes), our aircraft flying each candidate heading for 5 minutes with no turn lag, independent intruders, and timings measured in your browser's JavaScript, not in the competition's Python. It illustrates the structure of the idea, not the speed or accuracy we would get in the real pipeline. "Most urgent" ranks intruders by the closest any candidate heading would come to them under a straight-line forecast.

7How it could fit with what we are doing

The competition says the solution must be reinforcement-learning-based, not a hand-engineered classical controller or a pure search/optimiser. So the sensible reading is: use CSO ideas to improve what the RL agent sees, is rewarded for and learns from, rather than to replace it. Four options, from safest to most uncertain:

A · Observation

A fixed-size risk profile as features

Replace "nearest obstacle and nearest intruder with CPA features" by the vector from section 6: conflict probability per candidate heading, summed over all intruders. Same size for 1 or 10 intruders, so it plugs into our tile coding or a neural network. Risk: needs a predictor for bends; a few milliseconds per step.

B · Reward

Expected risk instead of rare events

Today the conflict penalty only fires when separation is already lost (rare, sparse). A dense term based on the expected conflict cost gives credit earlier. Potential-based shaping keeps the best policy unchanged. Risk: badly scaled shaping can distort behaviour; test against the scored metrics.

C · Training signal

Score every heading from the same state

Because single-agent intruders ignore us, a logged intruder path lets us compute the cost of every heading, not just the one sampled, a richer signal than policy gradient's single sampled action (my reading, not a claim from the literature). Could be an auxiliary loss or a warm start. Risk: only one-step; our own future state still matters. Untested.

D · Safety layer

A risk-aware filter around the RL action

Nudge the RL heading to the nearest low-risk one. Easy to build and the most effective in practice. Risk: it edges toward the "classical controller" the rules exclude. Ask the organisers before relying on it (contact details are in COMPETITION.md).

What I would test first

  1. Extend our testbed from 1 to about 3–10 intruders (the generator already makes them).
  2. Compare, at equal training budget: our current nearest-1 + CPA features against option A. Measure intrusion rate, goal rate and compute time per step on the same 100 scenarios.
  3. Only if A helps, try B and C. Leave D until the rules question is answered.
Be realistic about the payoff in the single-agent track. The data in section 5 says a plain straight-line check already catches nearly all upcoming conflicts. The headroom for better prediction is small; the real value of this view is the scalable representation (sections 6 and 7A) and a graded risk instead of a yes/no. The prediction side matters more in the multi-agent track, where intent is genuinely uncertain, but that is also where the exogeneity assumption fails.

Questions to take back to your professor

  1. By scalable, do you mean computation per decision as the number of obstacles grows, sample complexity, or the size of the learned model? (I assumed the first.)
  2. Is the uncertainty you have in mind about obstacle motion (intent, bends), or about sensing noise?
  3. Is a model-based risk estimate inside an RL pipeline acceptable under the competition's "RL-based" rule? Would a risk-aware filter be?
  4. For multi-agent: how would you handle other agents that react to our decisions, given CSO assumes they do not?
